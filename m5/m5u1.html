Unit 1

Collaborative discussion 1:


When reviewing the case study from ACM on Accessibility in software development it painted a picture of software development not always taking accessibility into account (Horton, 2018). The case study outlines how a software company releases a web-based tool that uses hidden controls making it inaccessible for blind or mobility impaired users (Horton, 2028). The case study reflects how leadership dismissed the need for accessibility through stating “How many blind people use our product, anyway?” thus dismissing the needs of some users (Horton, 2018). The release violated several areas of the ACM code of ethics. The following were the violations:

Principle 1.1- by failing to fix issues the release limited accessibility for disabled users.
Principle 1.2- the release caused harm to users due to the lack of accessibility.
Principle 1.4- The publication led to discrimination against disabled people.
Principle 2.3- The project and team did not follow the companies own accessibility policy.

The release also went against several other laws and ethical agreements. For example, the release violated the BCS code of conduct (2022) as the software release did not adhere to the BCS guidance on acting without discrimination, avoiding injuring others and to act with due care and diligence (BCS, 2022). As well as violating the BCS guidance the release also violated the Equality act (2010) that states that workplaces should “make ‘reasonable adjustments’ to avoid you being put at a disadvantage compared to non-disabled people in the workplace”. the company did not make reasonable adjustments to suit the needs of its disabled employees in the case. ACAS (2024) states that one way to make a workplace more accessible is to ensure “the tools, systems, and technology people use” are made accessible as well as the physical building and equipment. It is important that workplaces consider the needs of all employees at all times to avoid this type of discrimination occurring.

Ethics activity



When it comes to generative AI there are a vast array of benefits which are counterweighted by many negatives. I shall discuss both areas starting with the positives followed by the negatives and my ideas for how legislation should be adjusted to deal with the AI revolution of the 21st century.
AI undoubtedly has achieved many positives for example the use of AI for medical imaging (IBM, 2024) and how it has improved the accuracy and speed of medical image analysis (Stewart, 2024). These advancements can vastly improve global health and healthcare. AI can also be used to assist with the development of crops that are resistant to our changing climate allowing for stable food production in countries impacted by adverse weather conditions (Ali, 2025). AI can also be used to assist with the care of the elderly and vulnerable with AI being integrated into robots used for care (Padhan, 2023). these benefits allow AI to be utilised in a manner that will assist people and improve the lives of many people. These areas I believe are ethical many of which overlap with those outlined by Deckard (2023).
AI still has its misuse cases for example to use AI to generate images or videos without people’s permission or knowledge. This is an occurrence that is becoming more prevalent a recent example was from South Korea in the Nth room case (Mackenzie, 2024). More than 500 schools and universities were involved in the scandal that involved school children using AI to create fake sexually explicit images of their classmates. This is a prime example of how the ethics surrounding AI can be forgotten or overlooked especially when it comes to generative AI being available for public use. There is also a growing concern around AI and its use within education settings by students. For example, as a teacher myself, I have seen a rise is students attempting to pass AI generated work off as their own. With the widespread use of AI there are also concerns around how AI is impacting the environment (UNEP, 2024). This concern is often around AI’s massive water consumption for cooling purposes with it estimated that by 2027 AI water consumption could reach that of the country of Denmark (Li, 2025). This environmental impact is often cited as one of the main arguments against AI’s mainstream use.

According to the article by Correa et al (2023) states that an international consensus has been reached when it comes to some areas surrounding AI. These are the importance of: transparency, security, justice, privacy and accountability. These areas need focus and refinement to ensure that AI is legislated in a uniform way globally. If for example one country uphold certain AI standards that are not upheld by other this could cause the standard to be ignored. The lack of a strong and clear global consensus when it comes to AI’s use and implementation could create issues around what AI can be used to do.
In my opinion I feel that the regulations around AI need to be tighter as in some instances AI can be used to work around GDPR laws and data protection laws (2016) and information can be gathered and used without a person’s permission as in the Nth room case. I feel AI for public consumption should be heavily regulated. Examples of governments moving in a positive direction in terms of regulation can be found in the EU. The EU AI Act (2024) categorised uses of AI into different risk categorises with some uses being deemed prohibited or high risk thus requiring strict permission. This approach limits the scope of AI so that it doesn’t impact the rights of citizens. Overall, I feel regulation should have been created before AI was widely introduced as it would have been easier to set limit before making it available than attempting to rules later. The topic itself will impact the computer science field as AI has become divisive in the last couple of years with some that believe it is wholly beneficial and other claiming the contrary that it is wholly negative. I feel with earlier implementation of regulation opinions on AI would be less polarised.
